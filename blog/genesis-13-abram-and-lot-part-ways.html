18 Verses, 2 Herds, 1 Choice, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 13 Explained, Abram and Lot Part Ways for You

Here is the whole chapter in one line: Abram gives his nephew Lot first pick of the land to end a fight and keep peace in their house, and Lot takes the rich green plain toward Sodom — the best grass in sight, but a hard place to keep a house safe.

Here is the whole chapter in one line: Abram gives his nephew Lot first pick of the land to end a fight and keep peace in their house, and Lot takes the rich green plain toward Sodom — the best grass in sight, but a hard place to keep a house safe.

Two herdsmen are shouting at each other in the dust, staffs raised, while the sheep push and scatter around their feet. It is the third fight this week. The grass is thin, the well is low, and there are too many mouths for this one patch of land. Up the hill, two men watch: Abram, and his nephew Lot, whom he carried out of Ur as a son of the house. The men below work for them, so the fight below is theirs. Abram could end it with rank. He is the elder, the one God called, the one with the promise. He could point Lot to the thin hills and keep the good grass for his own flocks, and no one could call it theft. What he does instead is the hinge of this whole chapter, and one of the kindest acts in the book. This post walks that parting: why the land could not hold them, what Abram offered, what Lot's eyes chose, and what God said to Abram when he stood alone.

The short answer:

Genesis 13 brings Abram up out of Egypt, very rich in cattle, silver, and gold, and back to the altar near Bethel where he called on the name of the LORD. Lot, who went with him, also has flocks, herds, and tents, and the land cannot bear them both, so strife breaks out between their herdsmen. Abram asks Lot to part from him in peace and gives him the first choice of the land. Lot lifts up his eyes, sees the well watered plain of Jordan, and chooses it, moving east and pitching his tent toward Sodom. Abram stays in Canaan. Then God tells Abram to lift up his eyes and look in all four directions, promises him all the land he sees and seed like the dust of the earth, and tells him to walk through the land. Abram moves his tent to the plain of Mamre in Hebron and builds an altar there.

Where did Abram go first out of Egypt?

Verse 1: "And Abram went up out of Egypt, he, and his wife, and all that he had, and Lot with him, into the south." Went up is the book's own word for the trip from Egypt to Canaan, which climbs from the low river land to the hills. He comes out with all he had, and with Sarai at his side, Pharaoh's gifts and God's guard both behind him. Verse 2: "And Abram was very rich in cattle, in silver, and in gold." The Egypt trip that shamed his faith still left him rich. The book does not hide that either. Grace in this tale is not a wage paid for good work. It is a gift that lands on a flawed man.

Verses 3 and 4 trace his road by stages back to Bethel, "unto the place where his tent had been at the beginning, between Bethel and Hai; unto the place of the altar, which he had made there at the first: and there Abram called on the name of the LORD." Hai is also called Ai. Before any new thing happens in this chapter, Abram goes back to the old altar. He returns to the last place where his walk with God was clean and whole, and he calls on the name of the LORD again, out loud, as he did at first. If you have ever had to go back to a good place you left, to start right again, you know this road. Abram's first move after Egypt is not a new plan. It is a return.

Think of it like a plumb line in a builder's hand. A plumb line is a weight on a string that shows what is truly straight down. When a wall starts to lean, the builder does not guess. He goes back to the line and checks the wall against it. Bethel is Abram's plumb line: the altar where he last stood straight. Every wise restart in this book works that way. You go back to where the line was true, and you build from there.

Why could the land not bear them both?

Verse 5: "And Lot also, which went with Abram, had flocks, and herds, and tents." Lot has grown too. He came out of Haran as a nephew in Abram's train, and now he is a rich man in his own right, with herds and a house of tents. Verse 6: "And the land was not able to bear them, that they might dwell together: for their substance was great, so that they could not dwell together." Substance means all their goods and beasts. The land here is hill country: thin grass, few wells. Two great herds on one patch means the grass is eaten to the root and the wells are drunk dry by noon.

Verse 7: "And there was a strife between the herdmen of Abram's cattle and the herdmen of Lot's cattle: and the Canaanite and the Perizzite dwelled then in the land." Strife means open quarrel, the shouting match at the well where this post began. Herdsmen fight because herdsmen must: a man whose beasts go thirsty has failed his master. And the last line of the verse adds a sharp note. The Canaanite and the Perizzite were watching. The peoples of the land could see the house of the promise at war with itself over grass. A family quarrel is never as private as the family thinks.

Here is the plain shape of the trouble. Nothing here is sin in the crude sense. No one stole. No one lied. The herds grew, the land did not, and growth itself split the camp. That is worth sitting with, because many a house and many a church has split on the same rock: not evil, just too much life for one small field, and no wise word spoken in time. Abram speaks the wise word. That is the next move, and it is the turn of the chapter.

What did Abram offer Lot, and why was it so bold?

Verse 8: "And Abram said unto Lot, Let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee, and between my herdmen and thy herdmen; for we be brethren." I pray thee is an old, soft way of saying please. We be brethren means we are brothers, kin, one house. Abram starts with the bond, not the blame. He does not count whose herdsman swung first. He names the thing worth saving: the kinship itself.

Verse 9: "Is not the whole land before thee? separate thyself, I pray thee, from me: if thou wilt take the left hand, then I will go to the right; or if thou depart to the right hand, then I will go to the left." Left hand and right hand, for a man facing east, mean north and south. Abram offers Lot the whole land to pick from, and binds himself to take the rest. Now feel the weight of that offer. Abram is the elder. Abram is the one God called by name. Abram holds the promise of this very land, given at Sichem in the last chapter. By every rule of that world, Lot should wait to be placed. Instead the promised man opens his hand and says: you choose first.

Why could he do that? Because Abram's wealth did not rest on grass. His wealth rested on a word: "Unto thy seed will I give this land." A man who is sure God will keep him can afford to be soft in a land dispute. A man who thinks his life hangs on one good field must fight for the field. Think of two brothers at a table with one loaf between them, and the elder pushes the loaf across and says, take what you want, because he knows the house has a full store room behind it that the younger has not seen. Abram had seen the store room. It was a promise, spoken by God, at an altar Abram built with his own hands.

What did Lot see when he lifted up his eyes?

Verse 10: "And Lot lifted up his eyes, and beheld all the plain of Jordan, that it was well watered every where, before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, even as the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt, as thou comest unto Zoar." Lifted up his eyes means he looked out with care and weighed what he saw. And what he saw was good: a plain with water in every part, green like the garden of the LORD, Eden itself, and rich like Egypt along its river. Lot chose with his eyes, and his eyes did not lie to him. The plain truly was the best grass and water in sight. If the choice were only about flocks, Lot chose well.

But read the verse again and mark the dark thread sewn into it: "before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah." The writer, looking back from later years, tells you the end of that green plain while Lot is still gazing at it. The finest view in the chapter carries its own warning label. Verse 11: "Then Lot chose him all the plain of Jordan; and Lot journeyed east: and they separated themselves the one from the other." East, in this book, is a loaded direction. Adam went east out of Eden. Cain went east. The builders of Babel journeyed from the east. The text never scolds the compass, but the pattern is there for the slow reader: east is the way men walk when they walk by sight.

Verse 12: "Abram dwelled in the land of Canaan, and Lot dwelled in the cities of the plain, and pitched his tent toward Sodom." Toward is the saddest word in the chapter. Lot did not move into Sodom that day. He pitched toward it, one camp closer than a man of Abram's house should camp. Verse 13 tells you why the word lands so hard: "But the men of Sodom were wicked and sinners before the LORD exceedingly." Exceedingly means beyond measure. The green plain was real. So was the rot at its edge. Lot priced the grass and never priced the neighbors, and a man's neighbors shape his house more than his grass does. His herds would eat well. His children would grow up at the gate of Sodom.

Here is your read-through checklist for the chapter, so you can trace the parting yourself with your Bible open:

Checklist:

  • Verses 1–4: Abram goes up out of Egypt, very rich, and returns to the altar at Bethel to call on the name of the LORD
  • Verses 5–7: Lot's substance is also great, the land cannot bear them both, and strife breaks out between the herdsmen
  • Verses 8–9: Abram asks for no strife, for we be brethren, and gives Lot the first choice of the land
  • Verses 10–11: Lot lifts up his eyes, chooses the well watered plain of Jordan, and journeys east
  • Verses 12–13: Lot pitches his tent toward Sodom, whose men were wicked exceedingly
  • Verses 14–17: God tells Abram to lift up his eyes in all four directions, and promises the land and seed as the dust
  • Verse 18: Abram removes his tent to the plain of Mamre in Hebron, and builds an altar unto the LORD

What did God say to Abram after Lot left?

This is the part of the chapter a fast read skips, and it is the crown of it. Verse 14: "And the LORD said unto Abram, after that Lot was separated from him, Lift up now thine eyes, and look from the place where thou art northward, and southward, and eastward, and westward." Mark the timing: after that Lot was separated from him. God did not speak while Abram was giving the land away. He spoke after, to a man standing alone on a ridge, watching his nephew's tents shrink east. And He uses Lot's own act as His opening word: lift up thine eyes. Lot lifted his eyes and took a plain. Abram is told to lift his eyes and receive a world.

Verse 15: "For all the land which thou seest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed for ever." All the land, in all four directions, not the half Lot left behind. The man who let his nephew pick first is given everything his eyes can hold, north, south, east, and west, the east included. Verse 16: "And I will make thy seed as the dust of the earth: so that if a man can number the dust of the earth, then shall thy seed also be numbered." Dust is the book's way of saying a count no man can finish. Try it some day: take one handful of dry earth, let it run through your fingers, and count the grains. You cannot. That is the count God sets on the seed of a man whose wife is barren and whose house has just split in two. The promise does not shrink when Abram's camp does. It grows.

Verse 17: "Arise, walk through the land in the length of it and in the breadth of it; for I will give it unto thee." Breadth means width. God does not leave Abram standing on the ridge with a view. He sends him walking, the length and the width, foot by foot, the way a man walks a field that is his. A view is a promise seen. A walk is a promise handled. Then verse 18: "Then Abram removed his tent, and came and dwelt in the plain of Mamre, which is in Hebron, and built there an altar unto the LORD." Mamre is known for great oak trees, shade in the hill country. Abram's answer to the widest promise yet is the same as ever: a tent, and an altar. He owns no acre. He builds no city. He thanks God and stays a guest, sure the deed will come in God's time.

Here is the cost beat of this chapter, and it falls on both men in turn. Lot gained the best land in sight and lost ground he could not see: peace of house, the guard of Abram's camp, and a safe place to raise his seed. Within a few chapters he will be a captive carried off by kings, and later a man fleeing a burning city with nothing. Abram lost the best grass, the easy water, and the company of the nephew he raised, and he stood alone on a ridge for it. What he gained was spoken, not seized: all the land, in every direction, for ever. A careless read calls Lot the winner of this chapter, because he got the green plain. The book's own ledger says the man who gave away the choice walked off the ridge owning everything, and the man who took the best of it was already walking toward Sodom.

What is Genesis 13's bottom line?

Come back to the ridge. Two men stand above the dust and the shouting, and one of them says please, we are brethren, you pick first. The bottom line is this: the man who holds God's promise does not have to grab. Abram could give Lot the first choice because his life did not hang on one field; it hung on a word God had spoken and would speak again, wider, the moment the ridge went quiet. Lot chose by his eyes and priced only what he could see. Abram chose by his ears, by the voice he trusted, and was given more than eyes can take in at once. When the land cannot bear you both, when the strife starts at the well, the Abram way still works: guard the kinship, open your hand, and let God do the giving. He gives in four directions.
