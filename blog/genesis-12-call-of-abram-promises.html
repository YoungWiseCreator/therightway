7 Promises, 75 Years, 2 Altars, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 12 Explained, the Call of Abram for You

Abram is 75 years old, and he must leave his father's house for a land he has never seen, staking his whole life on seven promises from a God he cannot see.

Abram is 75 years old, and he must leave his father's house for a land he has never seen, staking his whole life on seven promises from a God he cannot see.

Abram's hand is still on the tent rope when the word comes back to him, clear as the first time: go. He is seventy and five years old, standing in the yard at Haran, and the men of his house have stopped their work to watch him. If he lets go of the rope and turns back inside, this day ends like every day before it. If he pulls the tent down, there is no going back.

His father Terah lies buried in this town. His wife Sarai stands near the door with no child at her side, and the years when a child might have come are long past. Behind Abram is all he knows: kin who share his name, graves he can visit, trade he can count on, and a house that works. In front of him is a road west that he has never walked, to a land God has not named. No map. No deed. No son to carry his name if the road takes him.

And the voice does not soften the call to make it easy to take. Leave your country. Leave your kindred. Leave your father's house. Go to a land I will show you as you go. Then, on the far side of that going, God sets seven promises: a great nation from a man with no child, a great name for a man told to leave his father's name behind, and every family on earth blessed through him.

So the old man stands there with the rope in his hand and two lives in front of him. Stay, and he keeps the safe house, the known road, and a name that ends with him. Go, and he stakes what is left of his life on a word he cannot check, from a God he cannot see. One pull on that rope will tell his whole house which life he has picked, and Sarai, Lot, and every servant in that yard are waiting to see which way his hand moves.

The short answer:

Genesis 12 opens with God's call to Abram: leave your country, your kindred, and your father's house, and go to a land God will show him. God promises to make of him a great nation, to bless him, to make his name great, to bless those who bless him and curse those who curse him, and to bless all families of the earth through him. Abram goes at seventy and five years old, with Sarai, Lot, and all they have. In Canaan, God appears to him at Sichem and promises the land to his seed, and Abram builds two altars, at Sichem and near Bethel. Then a famine drives him down to Egypt, where fear leads him to call Sarai his sister. Pharaoh takes her, God plagues Pharaoh's house, and Pharaoh sends Abram away with his wife and all he has.

What exactly did God tell Abram to leave?

Verse 1: "Now the LORD had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee." Had said is past tense. The call came before this page opens; some of it may have come back in Ur, before Terah's house ever set out. Kindred means his wider kin, the clan. Father's house means his own close house and its ways. Count the three cuts: country, the land he knows; kindred, the people he knows; father's house, the roof and name he was born under. Each cut goes deeper than the one before. Land, then clan, then home.

And see what is not in the verse. No land is named. "A land that I will shew thee" means a land I will show you, later, as you go. Abram is not told where. He is told Who is sending him, and that is all he gets. Think of a man told to drive at night with the guide riding beside him, who will say turn here, turn there, but will not hand over the map. The trust is not in the road. The trust is in the rider. That is the shape of this call from its first verse: not a plan Abram could weigh, but a voice Abram could follow or refuse.

What did God promise, promise by promise?

Verses 2 and 3 hold the promise, and it is worth counting on your fingers, the way a child counts a gift list. "And I will make of thee a great nation." One. "And I will bless thee." Two. "And make thy name great." Three. "And thou shalt be a blessing." Four. "And I will bless them that bless thee." Five. "And curse him that curseth thee." Six. "And in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed." Seven. Seven promises, and every one of them starts with God's "I will" or rests on it. Abram brings nothing to this list but his going.

Hold promise three up against the last chapter. At Babel, men said, "let us make us a name," and lost it in one day of mixed speech. Here God says to one man, "I will make thy name great," and that name is still spoken in every land on earth. The book wants you to feel that contrast in your chest. A name grabbed is a name lost. A name given is a name kept. And promise seven is the widest line in the book so far: not Abram's house only, but all families of the earth, blessed in him. The man with no child is told his seed will carry a gift to every family alive. The promise is as wide as the scattering of Babel, and it runs through one barren house.

Now do the hard math the chapter sets in front of you. Verse 4: "Abram was seventy and five years old when he departed out of Haran." Sarai, we learned in the last chapter, was barren; she had no child. A great nation is promised to a man of seventy and five with a wife who cannot bear. The text does not hide this or rush past it. It puts the age in the same breath as the going. The promise does not rest on what Abram's house can do. It rests on what God said He will do, and the gap between the two is the space this whole book will live in.

Who went, and what did they carry?

Verse 4 goes on: "So Abram departed, as the LORD had spoken unto him; and Lot went with him." Lot is his dead brother Haran's son, the nephew Abram has carried since Ur. Verse 5: "And Abram took Sarai his wife, and Lot his brother's son, and all their substance that they had gathered, and the souls that they had gotten in Haran; and they went forth to go into the land of Canaan; and into the land of Canaan they came." Substance means goods: flocks, tents, tools, wealth. Souls here means the people of the house, servants and their families. This was no lone walk. It was a whole house on the road, hundreds of miles on foot, with herds that can only move as fast as the slowest lamb.

Think of it like a family that sells its shop, packs two carts to the rails, and drives toward a town they have never seen, because the head of the house heard a call he could not shake. The neighbors would call it mad. Some days the family might call it mad too, at a dry well, in the heat. The text gives us none of that talk. It gives us one clean line: Abram departed, as the LORD had spoken unto him. The going itself is the faith. He did not wait to feel sure. He went, and the feelings had to catch up on the road.

What happened at Sichem and Bethel?

Verse 6: Abram "passed through the land unto the place of Sichem, unto the plain of Moreh. And the Canaanite was then in the land." Sichem is a town in the hill country, and the plain of Moreh is known for a great oak tree, a place where men met and taught. Note the plain note at the end: the Canaanite was then in the land. The promised land was not empty. Other peoples lived in it, farmed it, and held it. God did not promise Abram a blank field. He promised him a land with other men's fences already on it, and Abram would live in it as a guest for the rest of his days.

Verse 7: "And the LORD appeared unto Abram, and said, Unto thy seed will I give this land: and there builded he an altar unto the LORD, who appeared unto him." This is the first time the book says God appeared to a man in this way, face to face in some form the eye could take. And the promise grows a new part: the land itself, to his seed. Seed means his children and their children after them, the line that does not yet exist. Abram's answer is an altar: a pile of stones where gifts are offered and God is thanked. He builds the first altar in Canaan on a promise, in a land other men hold, for children he does not have. That is the man's whole life in one act.

Verse 8: he moves on to a mountain east of Bethel, pitches his tent, and builds a second altar, "and called upon the name of the LORD." Called upon the name means he prayed out loud, praised, and told God's name to any who would hear. Two altars in one short chapter mark Abram's road the way cairns mark a trail: here God spoke; here I thanked Him. Then verse 9: "And Abram journeyed, going on still toward the south." Still a tent man. Still moving. The promise has a land, but Abram has no deed to one foot of it yet, and he keeps walking.

Here is your read-through checklist for the chapter, so you can trace the steps yourself with your Bible open:

Checklist:

  • Verses 1–3: the call to leave country, kindred, and father's house, and the seven promises
  • Verses 4–5: Abram departs Haran at seventy and five years old, with Sarai, Lot, and all their substance
  • Verses 6–7: at Sichem, God appears and promises the land to Abram's seed; Abram builds an altar
  • Verse 8: near Bethel, a second altar, and Abram calls upon the name of the LORD
  • Verse 9: Abram journeys on toward the south, still in a tent
  • Verses 10–13: famine drives him to Egypt, and fear shapes the sister plan
  • Verses 14–20: Pharaoh takes Sarai, plagues fall, and Pharaoh sends Abram away with all he has

Why did Abram go down to Egypt, and what went wrong?

Verse 10: "And there was a famine in the land: and Abram went down to Egypt to sojourn there; for the famine was grievous in the land." A famine is a long dry time when crops fail and food runs short. Grievous means heavy and hard to bear. Sojourn means to stay for a while as a guest, not to settle. Note what the text does not say: it does not say God told him to go, and it does not say God told him not to. The road south was a man's own call in a hard year, and the book lets it stand as that. Egypt had a great river and grain when Canaan had none. Hungry men walk toward food. That part needs no blame.

The fear comes next, and Abram names it himself. Verses 11 to 13: he tells Sarai that she is a fair woman, that the Egyptians will see her, and will kill him to take her, so she should say she is his sister, "that it may be well with me for thy sake; and my soul shall live because of thee." Fair means good to look at. The plan is plain: hide the marriage, save the husband. There was a grain of fact in it, for Sarai was his kin, his father's daughter by another mother, as Abram himself says later in this book. But a half fact used to hide the whole truth is a bent tool, and it bends on the man who uses it. The one who was told "I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee" is now scheming to keep his own skin safe, at his wife's cost.

Here is the same fear in a plain picture. A man is given a strong escort for a road, a guard who has never lost a charge. Halfway down the road, at the sight of rough men at a gate, he slips away from his guard and tries to talk his way through on a made-up tale. The guard was the safe part. The tale is the risk. Abram had the best escort ever promised to a man, the word of God Himself, and at Egypt's gate he stepped out from behind it and used a tale instead. The chapter does not scold him in so many words. It just shows you what the tale bought.

How did God guard the promise in Egypt?

Verse 15: Pharaoh's princes saw Sarai and praised her to Pharaoh, "and the woman was taken into Pharaoh's house." Verse 16: Abram was treated well for her sake, with sheep, oxen, asses, servants, and camels. The plan worked, in the worst way. Abram grew rich in Egypt while his wife sat in a king's house under a false name. Then verse 17: "And the LORD plagued Pharaoh and his house with great plagues because of Sarai Abram's wife." Plagues here are hard blows of sickness or harm sent by God. God does in Egypt what Abram failed to trust Him to do: He guards the promise. The seed must come through Sarai. No king's house will swallow that line, even when Abram's own fear has handed it over.

Pharaoh does the sums and calls Abram in. Verse 18: "What is this that thou hast done unto me? why didst thou not tell me that she was thy wife?" The king of Egypt, who does not know Abram's God, puts the honest question the chapter has been waiting for. Verse 20: Pharaoh commands his men, and "they sent him away, and his wife, and all that he had." Abram leaves Egypt with Sarai restored and his goods kept, richer than he came, and taught a lesson he did not pay full price for. Grace is the only word for that ledger. The promise held, not because the man held it, but because God did.

Here is the cost beat of this chapter. If Pharaoh had kept Sarai, the line of the promise would have ended in a king's house in Egypt, in the first year of the call. Every family of the earth blessed in Abram rests, in this chapter, on a wife in danger and a God who steps in when the man's faith steps back. A careless read can miss how near the loss was, because the text stays calm. Read it slow: the father of a great nation had no nation, no land, and for a stretch of days in Egypt, no wife at his side either. All he had was the word. The word was enough, but the chapter makes sure you see that the word, and not the man, is what carried the weight.

What is Genesis 12's bottom line?

Come back to the yard at Haran. Abram is 75 years old, and he must leave his father's house for a land he has never seen, staking his whole life on seven promises from a God he cannot see. An old man with no map, a barren wife, and a dead father's unfinished road sets out because God spoke. The bottom line is this: the call of God does not wait for your life to look ready. Abram was seventy and five, his house had no child, and the land he was promised was held by other men. None of that slowed the promise by one day. And when the same man faltered in Egypt, the promise did not fall with his fear. God kept what He said He would keep. Your part is Abram's part at his best: hear the word, go, build your altars where He speaks, and call on His name as you walk. The name men tried to grab at Babel, God gives here, freely, to a man who simply went.
