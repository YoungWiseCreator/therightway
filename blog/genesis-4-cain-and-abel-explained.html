2 Brothers, 2 Offerings, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 4 Explained, Cain and Abel Your Family Reads Slow

Genesis 3 ended at a gate, with a guard and a sword of fire.

Genesis 3 ended at a gate, with a guard and a sword of fire. Genesis 4 opens in a home. A baby is born. Then a second baby is born. By the end of the chapter, one brother is dead, one brother is sent away, a city is built, and a new baby is born who starts the line that leads to Noah. Read it slow, and you can see each step plain.

The short answer:

Genesis 4 tells the story of Cain and Abel, the first two sons of Adam and Eve. Both brothers bring a gift to God. God receives Abel and his gift, but not Cain and his gift. Cain grows angry. God warns him that sin crouches at the door and must be ruled. Cain kills Abel in the field anyway. God curses Cain from the ground, yet also sets a mark on him to guard his life. Cain builds the first city. Then Seth is born, and men begin to call on the name of the LORD.

What the video shows: The video walks through the story of Cain and Abel from Genesis 4, and it starts with their names. Cain's name sounds like the Hebrew word for gotten, or acquired, from Eve's own words in verse 1: I have gotten a man from the LORD. Abel's name means breath, a life that is short, like a puff of air. The video then goes back to the garden and shows how want and desire can be aimed the wrong way. It looks at the two gifts the brothers bring, at God's warning in verse 7, and at the sad day in the field in verse 8. It ends with later parts of the Bible that still speak of Abel's gift, and it shows that his faith is still named long after his short life ends.

Who are Cain and Abel, and what work does each one do?

Verse 1 says Adam knew Eve his wife, and she bore Cain. Knew here is the old way the KJV says a husband and wife came together and a child was born. Eve says, I have gotten a man from the LORD. The name Cain sounds like the Hebrew word for gotten. His name holds his mother's joy. She has a son, and she gives God the credit for him.

Verse 2 says she bore his brother Abel. The name Abel means breath. Breath is the soft air that goes in and out of your chest. It does not last long. You can not hold it in your hand. The name fits his life. His life is short. But his name is still in the book thousands of years later. A short life can still be a true life.

Then the verse tells you their work. Abel was a keeper of sheep. A keeper of sheep is a man who feeds sheep, leads them to grass and water, and guards them from wild beasts. Cain was a tiller of the ground. A tiller of the ground is a farmer. He breaks up the soil, plants seed, and waits for the crop to grow.

Both jobs are good jobs. The chapter does not say one job is bad and one job is good. A farmer feeds the world from the soil. A shepherd feeds the world from the flock. Both men work the ground God cursed in Genesis 3, the ground that now grows thorns and makes work hard. The split in this story is not farmer against shepherd. It comes later, at the gift, and then in the heart.

What do the two brothers bring to God?

Verses 3 to 5 say that in the process of time, both brothers brought a gift to the LORD. A gift brought to God like this is called an offering. An offering is a thing you bring to God to thank Him, to honor Him, or to draw near to Him. Cain brought fruit of the ground. Abel brought the firstlings of his flock and the fat of it.

Firstlings means the firstborn ones. The first lambs born to his sheep. The fat is the rich, best part of the meat. So Abel brought first and best. The text marks that. It names the firstlings. It names the fat. For Cain, the text just says fruit of the ground. It does not say first. It does not say best. Read slow and let that gap sit there. The text does not tell you all that was in Cain's heart. It shows you the two gifts side by side, and one is named first and best, and the other is not.

Then comes the hard line. The LORD had respect unto Abel and to his offering. Respect here means God received him and his gift with favor. But unto Cain and to his offering He had not respect. God did not receive Cain's gift in the same way.

Note the order. God looks at the man, then the gift. Abel and his offering. Cain and his offering. The gift and the giver are tied. God sees the heart that brings the gift, not just the thing in the hands.

Cain's face tells the next part. Cain was very wroth, and his countenance fell. Wroth means angry, hot with anger. Countenance is your face, the look on it. His face fell. You could see the anger and the hurt on him, like a cloud coming down over his eyes.

Here is a place to read with care. The chapter does not say God hated Cain. It does not say the door was shut. In the next breath, God comes and talks to Cain. He asks him a question. He gives him a warn and a way back. A gift not received is a sad day. But it is not the end of the story yet. What Cain does with his anger is the next step, and it is the step that counts most.

What does God say to Cain before the worst day?

Verses 6 and 7 are God's talk with Cain, and they come before any blood is shed. God asks, Why art thou wroth? And why is thy countenance fallen? Art thou is old words for are you. God knew why. The question is for Cain. It gives Cain a chance to say his hurt out loud, to God, while there is still time.

Then God gives him a clear rule. If thou doest well, shalt thou not be accepted? Do well, and you will be received. The door is not shut. The way back is plain. Do the right thing, and the gift and the giver can be received.

Then the warn. If thou doest not well, sin lieth at the door. Lieth means it lies there, crouched low, like a wild beast waiting to spring. Sin is a wrong choice against God's word. God says sin wants Cain: unto thee shall be his desire. And God gives the charge: thou shalt rule over him. Rule over it. Do not let it rule you.

This is the hardest idea in the chapter, so hear it twice.

First, think of a dog at a door. A stray dog sits at your step all day. It does not come in on its own. It waits. If you open the door and call it, it comes in and makes itself at home. If you keep the door shut and shoo it off, it stays out. Sin in verse 7 is like that dog at the step. It is near. It wants in. But Cain holds the door. What he does next is his choice.

Second, think of a fire in dry grass. A small spark lands at the edge of a field. At first it is one tiny glow. One bucket of water ends it. But if a man stands and watches it, and feeds it with his anger, it grows tall and fast, and soon it takes the whole field and can not be called back. Cain's anger is that spark. Verse 7 is God handing him the bucket while the spark is still small.

Both pictures say the same true thing. God warned Cain in plain words, in time, with a way out. The warn is a mercy. No man in this chapter can say he was not told.

What happens in the field, and what does God ask Cain?

Verse 8 is one of the most sad verses in the book. Cain talked with Abel his brother. And it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him. Slew means killed. The talk in the field is not told to us. The text does not give us their words. It gives us the act. Cain rose up, and Abel died. This is the first killing in the Bible. A brother kills a brother.

Then God comes, as He came in the garden. Verse 9: Where is Abel thy brother? It is the same kind of question as in Genesis 3, when God called, Where art thou? God knows the answer. The question opens a door for Cain to tell the truth.

Cain shuts it. He says, I know not. That is a lie. He does know. Then he adds the line men still quote: Am I my brother's keeper? A keeper is one who guards and cares for. Cain uses the word for Abel's own job. Abel kept sheep. Cain asks if he must keep his brother. The words are cold. They push his brother's life off his own back.

God answers in verse 10. What hast thou done? The voice of thy brother's blood crieth unto me from the ground. Blood here stands for Abel's life, cut short. The ground that took his blood now speaks. Nothing is hid from God. The field had no crowd. No man saw. But God heard.

Verses 11 and 12 give the curse. Cain is cursed from the earth. The ground will no longer yield her strength to him. The farmer's soil will fight him now. And he will be a fugitive and a vagabond in the earth. A fugitive is a man who runs from place to place. A vagabond is a man with no home who wanders. Cain, who worked one field, must now wander all fields and rest in none.

Cain cries out in verse 13: My punishment is greater than I can bear. He fears that men who find him will kill him. Note what he does not say. He does not say he is sorry for Abel. His pain is for his own loss. The text shows you that too, plain, with no soft words over it.

What does the mark on Cain mean?

Verse 15 holds a thing many readers miss. God does not leave Cain to be killed by the first man who finds him. The LORD set a mark upon Cain, lest any finding him should kill him. Lest means so that it will not happen. And God says that whoever slays Cain, vengeance shall be taken on him sevenfold. Sevenfold means seven times over.

So what is the mark? Here the text is quiet, and we must be quiet with it. The KJV says God set a mark upon Cain. The Hebrew word is a word for a sign. The chapter does not say what the sign looked like. It does not say where on his body it was. It does not say it was a shape, a color, or a scar. Any man who tells you just what it looked like is adding to the text. We do not add. We say what it was for, because the text does say that: it was for guard. It kept men from killing Cain.

That is a strange mercy, and it is a real one. Cain killed his brother. Cain lied to God. And still God sets a guard on Cain's life. Judgment in this chapter is true and hard: curse, wandering, loss of the field. But God does not hand Cain over to the revenge of men. Life is still God's to guard, even the life of a man who took life.

Think of it like a fence around a field that is not yours to burn. The weeds in it are real. But the fence says: hands off, this is not yours to judge. God's mark is that fence on Cain. Men do not get to run the court of blood on their own. When you read this with your family, say both halves out loud. The curse is real. The guard is real.

What does Cain build, and who comes from his line?

Verse 16 says Cain went out from the presence of the LORD and dwelt in the land of Nod, on the east of Eden. Nod is a name tied to wandering. The wanderer lands in the land of wandering. Even there, life goes on. Verse 17 says Cain's wife bore a son, Enoch, and Cain built a city, and called the name of the city after his son, Enoch.

Stop at that fact. This is the first city in the Bible. A city is a place where many families live close, with walls or bounds, work split up, and a name over the gate. The first city is built by Cain, and it bears his son's name, not God's. Man now builds his own place to rest, since he can not rest in the field or in the garden. The city is skill. The city is also a sign: the wanderer tries to settle himself.

Then verses 18 to 22 run Cain's line down: Enoch, Irad, Mehujael, Methusael, Lamech. In Lamech's house you meet the start of many crafts. Jabal is the father of those who dwell in tents and keep cattle. Jubal is the father of all who handle the harp and the organ. An organ here is a pipe, a wind tool that makes music. Tubal-cain taught men to work brass and iron. Brass is a hard yellow metal. Iron is the strong gray metal tools and blades are made from.

See what the text shows you. Skill grows fast in Cain's line: herding, music, metal work. These crafts are not called sin. The line is gifted. But Lamech's part ends with a dark song. In verses 23 and 24, Lamech sings to his two wives, Adah and Zillah, that he has slain a man, and he claims a guard far past Cain's: if Cain is avenged sevenfold, Lamech will be avenged seventy and sevenfold. That is 7 stretched to 77. Lamech takes God's guard on Cain and turns it into a boast for his own killing. Real skill, real gifts, and a heart that sings about blood. The text does not hide either side.

How does the chapter end with hope?

The last two verses turn the page. Verse 25: Adam knew his wife again, and she bore a son, and called his name Seth. Eve says why: God hath appointed me another seed instead of Abel, whom Cain slew. Appointed means set in place, given on purpose. Seth's name sounds like that word appointed. Seed here means a child, one who comes from you and carries the line on.

Abel is gone. Cain is gone east. But the line does not end in the field. God gives a third son, and through Seth the story will run on to Noah, as Genesis 5 will show you name by name. Hope in this book is often a baby in a sad house. It was true at Seth's birth. Watch for it as you read on.

Verse 26 ends the chapter: to Seth a son is born, Enos, and then began men to call upon the name of the LORD. To call on the name of the LORD means to pray to God, to speak to Him by name, to seek Him out loud with other men. After a chapter of anger, blood, curse, and a boast song, the last line is prayer. Men begin to call on God again. That is the note the chapter leaves in your ear.

Read the whole chapter one more time, all 26 verses, with no stops. Count the turns: two births, two gifts, one warn, one field, one curse, one mark, one city, one song, one new birth, one first prayer. The shape is plain when you read it slow. And keep the rule of this site: if a claim is not in the chapter, do not add it to the chapter. The text is strong on its own.

The bottom line

Genesis 4 is the story of two brothers, two gifts, and one warn that came in time. Cain let anger rule him, and Abel died in the field. God cursed Cain from the ground, yet set a mark to guard his life. Cain built the first city, his line grew in skill, and then God gave Seth, and men began to call on the name of the LORD. Read all 26 verses slow, in order, and let the KJV text say what it says, no more and no less.
