300 Cubits Long, 50 Wide, 30 High, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 6 Explained, the Nephilim and Noah for You

Genesis 5 ended with a list that held.

Genesis 5 ended with a list that held. Genesis 6 opens with a world that does not hold. Men grow in number. Sin grows faster. God grieves that He made man. And in the middle of the worst report in the book so far, one line shines: Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD. Read this chapter slow. It is short, 22 verses, and every verse carries weight.

The short answer:

Genesis 6 tells why the flood came. Men multiplied on the earth. The sons of God took wives from the daughters of men, and giants, the Nephilim, were on the earth. God set a bound of 120 years. He saw that the wickedness of man was great, and that every thought of man's heart was only evil all the time. It grieved Him that He had made man. But Noah found grace. God made a covenant with Noah, gave him the plan of the ark, 300 cubits long, 50 wide, and 30 high, and Noah did all that God commanded him.

What the video shows: The video walks through Genesis 6 and the start of Genesis 7 in order, with the text on screen as it goes. It opens with the sons of God who left their place and the giants born on the earth in verses 1 to 4. Then it shows the world filled with violence, and God's grief that He made man, in verses 5 to 7. Next it shows Noah finding grace, and God giving him the size of the ark and the covenant in verses 8 to 21. It closes with Noah doing all that God commanded, and with the flood waters rising in Genesis 7, up over the hills, till only Noah and those with him in the ark were left. The whole arc of the chapter is there: sin, grief, grace, and a boat built on dry land.

Who are the sons of God and the Nephilim in verses 1 to 4?

The chapter opens wide. Verse 1: men began to multiply on the face of the earth, and daughters were born unto them. Multiply means to grow in number, fast. The long lives of Genesis 5 now show their fruit. The earth is filling up with people.

Verse 2: the sons of God saw the daughters of men that they were fair, and they took them wives of all which they chose. Fair means beautiful. Took wives means they married them, or took them as wives. The text gives no long talk about it. It states it in one line and moves on.

Who are the sons of God? Here we must read with care, because the chapter does not stop to tell us in so many words. The phrase sons of God is used in the book of Job for angels, God's heavenly servants, who came to stand before Him. Many readers, from very old times, have read Genesis 6 the same way: heavenly beings crossed a line God set, and took human wives. Other readers hold that the sons of God are the line of Seth, the line that called on God, and the daughters of men are from the line of Cain, and the sin is the two lines mixing till faith is lost. Still others read sons of God as proud rulers and kings who took any wife they wanted. All three reads are old. All three are held by men who love this book. What the chapter itself makes plain is the fruit: a world where the strong take what they want, and no line holds.

Verse 4 names the Nephilim. The KJV word is giants. There were giants in the earth in those days, and also after that, when the sons of God came in unto the daughters of men, and they bore children to them. The Hebrew word is Nephilim. The text calls their children the mighty men which were of old, men of renown. Mighty men means men of great strength and fame. Renown means a great name, a name all men know. These are the strong ones of the old world, the ones songs were sung about. The chapter does not praise them. It names them as part of a world about to be judged. Great strength and a great name are not the same as a good heart. Genesis 6 will prove that in 5 more verses.

Ancient copies back this part of the book. Pieces of Genesis were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, old copies kept in caves for about 2,000 years, and the scrolls also held writings about Enoch and the watchers that grow out of these very verses. The oldest copies we have tell the same strange story the KJV tells. The text has been kept with care.

What does God set, and what does God see, in verses 3 to 7?

Verse 3: God says, My spirit shall not always strive with man, for that he also is flesh: yet his days shall be an hundred and twenty years. Strive means to plead, to wrestle, to keep working on a man to turn him back. God will not plead with man without end. He sets a bound: 120 years. Readers take this two ways, and both are worth your ear. Some read it as the span of grace left before the flood, 120 years of warn while Noah builds and preaches. Others read it as a new cap on the life of man. The 900-year lives of Genesis 5 will not go on. After the flood, lives in Genesis shrink step by step toward 120 and below. The verse holds both ideas in one line: God sets a limit, in time and in years.

Then verses 5 to 7 give the worst report in the book so far. God saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth. Wickedness is evil done on purpose, harm chosen again and again. And that every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually. Imagination here means the plans and shapes a man's mind makes in the dark. Not just bad acts. Bad plans, all day, every day, with no good mixed in. The sin of Genesis 6 is not one bad day. It is a whole world whose inner life has gone rotten.

Verse 6 is one of the most strong verses in the Bible. And it repented the LORD that he had made man on the earth, and it grieved him at his heart. Repented here means God was sorry, full of grief, over what man had become. Grieved at his heart means the pain went to His core. Read it plain. The God who made man in His image looks at what man has made of himself, and His heart hurts.

Here is the hardest idea in the chapter, so hear it twice.

First, think of a man who builds a fine house with his own hands. He cuts each board. He fits each joint. He gives the house to his son. Years later he walks past and sees the roof torn off for fun, the walls kicked in, the rooms used to harm the town. His grief is not that the boards cost him money. His grief is love. He made a good thing, and he must watch it wreck itself. That is a small picture of verse 6.

Second, think of a potter at his wheel. A potter is a man who shapes wet clay into pots. If the clay will not hold its shape, if it sags and tears each time his hands lift it, he does not hate the clay. He grieves the pot that should have been. Then he takes the clay down and starts the work over, because the clay as it stands can not hold water for anyone. God's grief in verse 6 is that grief, and the flood is the work starting over, with one man's house saved through it.

Both pictures say the same thing. Verse 6 is not God caught off guard. It is God shown in the words of men, so we can feel the weight of it: sin does not just break rules. It grieves the heart of the One who made us.

Verse 7 says God will destroy man whom He created from the face of the earth, man and beast and bird, for it grieves Him that He made them. Destroy here is the word for blot out, to wipe off, like wiping a name off a wet slate. The whole living world that shares man's earth will share man's fall. That is the dark half of the chapter. Verse 8 is the light.

Why does Noah stand out in verses 8 to 10?

Verse 8 is five words in the KJV that carry the whole book: But Noah found grace in the eyes of the LORD. Grace means favor given free, a gift of kindness that is not earned by the one who gets it. This is the first time the word grace shows up in the Bible. It shows up here, in the worst chapter yet, pinned to one man's name.

Verse 9 tells you who Noah is. Noah was a just man and perfect in his generations, and Noah walked with God. Just means right and fair in how he dealt with God and men. Perfect here does not mean he never did a wrong thing in his life. It means whole, sound, not split — the same man in the dark as in the light. And he walked with God. You heard that phrase one chapter ago, about Enoch. Enoch walked with God and was taken. Noah walks with God and is kept through the flood. In a world where every heart plans evil all day, Noah walks the other way, in step with God, in his own house and his own town, where all can see.

Verse 10 names his house: Noah begat three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth. Begat means he became the father of. Three sons, the same three the end of Genesis 5 named. The list and the story meet here. Lamech hoped his baby would bring comfort in the toil under the curse. Now that baby is a grown man, the one just man in a rotten world, and God is about to speak to him.

Hold the contrast the chapter draws. On one side: the mighty men, the men of renown, the giants with great names. On the other side: one just man who walks with God. The chapter does not ask who is stronger. It shows you who is left standing when the waters come. Fame is loud. Grace is quiet. Grace wins.

What does God tell Noah about the earth and the ark?

Verses 11 to 13 say the earth was corrupt before God, and filled with violence. Corrupt means spoiled, gone bad all through, like milk that has turned in the whole jug, not just the top. Violence is the Hebrew word hamas: harm done to others by force, men hurting men as a way of life. God says to Noah: The end of all flesh is come before me, for the earth is filled with violence through them, and I will destroy them with the earth. All flesh means every living thing that breathes on land. The judgment is set. But God does not speak judgment to Noah and stop. He speaks judgment, then He speaks a plan.

Verse 14: Make thee an ark of gopher wood. An ark here is a great box built to float, a chest for keeping life safe. The KJV uses this same word ark for the box Moses's mother made in Exodus. Gopher wood is the Hebrew word gofer. No one today can say for sure which tree it was. The text does not tell us, and we do not guess past it. Noah is told to make rooms in the ark, and to pitch it within and without with pitch. Pitch is a thick, dark sap that seals wood and keeps water out. Rooms means stalls and parts split up inside, space for the kinds of beasts God will send.

Verse 15 gives the size, and the size is worth your math. The ark is 300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide, and 30 cubits high. A cubit is the span from a man's elbow to the tip of his middle finger, about 18 inches, about a foot and a half. Now run the counts. 300 times a foot and a half is 450 feet long. 50 times a foot and a half is 75 feet wide. 30 times a foot and a half is 45 feet high. That is a box longer than a football field and a half, and as high as a four-story house. This is no small boat. It is a huge floating chest, built to ride out a flood, not to sail. It has no sail and no rudder in the text. God does the steering. Noah does the building.

Verse 16 adds the rest of the plan. A window, one cubit, finished all around near the top. A door in the side of the ark. Three stories: lower, second, and third. Then verse 17: God will bring a flood of waters upon the earth, to destroy all flesh. Verse 18 turns the whole talk: But with thee will I establish my covenant. A covenant is a firm promise, a bond God makes and keeps. This is the first covenant named in the Bible. Noah, his sons, his wife, and his sons' wives will come into the ark. Verses 19 to 21: of every living thing, two of every sort will come to keep them alive, and Noah is to take food for all, and store it up. Verse 22 ends the chapter the best way a chapter can end: Thus did Noah; according to all that God commanded him, so did he.

Checklist:

  • Read Genesis 6:1-4 and name what the text says about the sons of God and the Nephilim, and what it does not say
  • Read Genesis 6:5-8 out loud and mark the two halves: man's wickedness, and Noah found grace
  • Run the ark math yourself: 300 x 1.5 feet long, 50 x 1.5 feet wide, 30 x 1.5 feet high
  • List the parts of God's plan in verses 14-21: wood, pitch, window, door, three stories, covenant, pairs, food
  • Check verse 22 and say in your own words how Noah answered all that God commanded

How should your family read Genesis 6 slow?

Read it in three parts, the way the chapter is built. First, verses 1 to 4, the strange start: sons of God, daughters of men, giants on the earth. Say out loud what the text says, and say out loud where it is quiet. Do not build big claims on the quiet parts.

Next, verses 5 to 10, the grief and the grace. Mark the worst line in the chapter, verse 5, every thought of man's heart only evil all the time. Then mark the best line, verse 8, Noah found grace. The whole Bible will run on those two rails: man at his worst, and God's grace finding a man anyway.

Last, verses 11 to 22, the plan. Read God's words to Noah slow. Note that God gives sizes, parts, and steps, and Noah's whole answer is to do them. Faith in this chapter is not a feeling. It is a man cutting wood to a size God gave, on dry land, for rain no one has seen fall like this. When your family talks it over, ask: what has God told us plain that we have not yet done? That is the Noah question, and it still stands.

Keep your notes in one place. Write the verse next to each fact. If a claim is not in the chapter, do not add it to the chapter. That rule keeps your read true, and it is the rule this whole site reads by: the Hebrew text, the oldest KJV, and the old copies among the Dead Sea Scrolls, read together, saying what they say and no more.

The bottom line

Genesis 6 is the chapter where the world breaks and grace first gets its name. Men filled the earth with violence, God's heart grieved, and a bound of 120 years was set. But Noah found grace, walked with God, and built the ark to the size God gave: 300 cubits long, 50 wide, 30 high. Read all 22 verses slow, in order, and let the KJV text say what it says, no more and no less.
