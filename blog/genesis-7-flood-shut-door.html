8 Souls, 40 Days of Rain, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 7 Explained, the Flood and the Shut Door for You

Genesis 6 ended with a big boat built and a hard word from God.

Genesis 6 ended with a big boat built and a hard word from God. Genesis 7 is the day the word came true. The rain starts in this chapter. The door shuts in this chapter. The water climbs over the hills in this chapter. And the chapter tells it all in plain counts: how many souls, how many days, how deep the water, and how long it stayed. Read it slow with us, verse by verse, in the King James text. A cubit, a word the chapter will use for depth, is the length from a man's elbow to the tip of his middle finger, about 18 inches, or a foot and a half. Keep that measure in mind. We will need it when the water rises.

The short answer:

Genesis 7 is the flood itself. God tells Noah to come into the ark with his house. The clean beasts come by sevens and the rest by twos. Seven days later the rain starts, on an exact day the chapter names. Rain falls 40 days and 40 nights. The waters rise 15 cubits over the mountains. All life on dry land dies. Noah and those with him live. The waters stay on the earth 150 days.

Who went into the ark, and how many souls were saved?

Count the people with me. The chapter names Noah. It names his wife. It names his three sons: Shem, Ham, and Japheth. It names the three wives of his sons. Now add them up. Noah is 1. His wife makes 2. Three sons make 5, because 2 + 3 = 5. Three wives make 8, because 5 + 3 = 8. Eight souls in all. The whole human race, at the end of this chapter, fits in one boat with room to spare.

Verse 1 gives the call: "Come thou and all thy house into the ark; for thee have I seen righteous before me in this generation." Look at the first word. God does not say "Go." He says "Come." Come is the word you use when you are already in the place and you call someone in to join you. The text lets you hear that God is, in a manner of speaking, already in the ark, and Noah comes in to Him. That is a small word with a big heart. The ark is not a place God sends Noah away to. It is a place God meets him in.

Righteous is the other word to slow down on. Righteous means right with God, a man whose life lines up with what God says. Noah is not called perfect. He is called righteous, and in the last chapter he was also called a man who walked with God. A walk is step by step, day by day. That is how Noah got here: not one big leap, but a long walk in the right direction, taken one step at a time, while the world around him walked the other way.

Why did the clean beasts come by sevens and the rest by twos?

Verse 2 says: "Of every clean beast thou shalt take to thee by sevens, the male and his female: and of beasts that are not clean by two, the male and his female." Verse 3 adds the birds: "Of fowls also of the air by sevens, the male and the female; to keep seed alive upon the face of all the earth."

Clean and unclean are God's own sorts. A clean beast is one God counts fit for His altar and, later in the law, fit for man's table. An unclean beast is one He does not. The chapter does not stop to list them. It just uses God's sorts, the way a cook uses the labels already on the jars.

Now do the math the text sets up. Twos are one pair: 1 male + 1 female = 2. Sevens are more. If a kind comes by sevens, there are spare lives of that kind on board. Why would the clean kinds need spares? The text gives one reason out loud: "to keep seed alive upon the face of all the earth." Seed here means the line of life that goes on after. A pair keeps a kind alive. Extra clean beasts do more than that. In the next chapter, Noah will build an altar and offer clean beasts to God. You cannot offer the only pair and still keep the kind alive. The sevens solve that. There are enough to offer, and enough left to breed. God counts ahead. The numbers in this chapter are not loose. They are planned, the way a wise man packs more food than the trip seems to need, because he knows what waits at the far end.

Think of it like a seed jar on a shelf. A farmer keeps seed corn in a jar, apart from the corn he eats. If he eats the seed corn, the meal is fine today and the field is bare next spring. Noah's ark is a seed jar for the whole earth. The pairs are the seed. The extra clean beasts are the part that can be used along the way and at the end, while the seed stays safe. That is why the counts differ. The plan is not just to live through the water. The plan is to start the world again on the far side of it.

What happened in the seven days before the rain?

Verse 4 sets the clock: "For yet seven days, and I will cause it to rain upon the earth forty days and forty nights; and every living substance that I have made will I destroy from off the face of the earth." Then verse 10: "And it came to pass after seven days, that the waters of the flood were upon the earth."

So Noah, his house, and all those animals sat in the ark for seven days with no rain. Seven days of waiting inside a boat on dry land. The text does not tell us what the neighbors said. It does not need to. You can hear it in your head. A man in a boat, in the dry, with the door still open, for a week.

Waiting is part of the walk. Noah did all that God said before the first drop fell, and then he waited for God to do what God said. Faith, in this chapter, is not just building. Faith is also sitting still in the boat you built, looking foolish, while the sky stays clear. If you are in a season where you have obeyed and nothing has moved yet, this chapter was written for you too. The rain was never in doubt. It was only on God's clock, not Noah's.

Then the chapter does a thing worth noting. It names the exact day. Verse 11: "In the six hundredth year of Noah's life, in the second month, the seventeenth day of the month, the same day were all the fountains of the great deep broken up, and the windows of heaven were opened." A year, a month, a day. The text keeps books like a careful clerk. This is not told the way men tell a tale that starts "once upon a time." It is told the way a man writes a log: on this day, this happened. You are meant to read it as an account of a real day in a real man's life.

Where did all the water come from?

The chapter names two sources, and it names them in one breath. "All the fountains of the great deep broken up, and the windows of heaven were opened." Water from below. Water from above. The deep is the great water under the earth, the sea beneath the ground that the old world knew was there, in wells and springs. Its fountains broke up, the way a dam breaks, all at once. The windows of heaven are the sky opened like a sluice gate, the way a man lifts the gate on a mill pond and the water pours through in a sheet.

Verse 12 gives the count: "And the rain was upon the earth forty days and forty nights." Forty days is 40 days and 40 nights of rain that does not stop. But hold the two numbers apart, because the chapter does. Rain fell for 40 days. The waters stayed on the earth for 150 days. 150 is much more than 40. 150 − 40 = 110. The water kept rising and standing long after the rain stopped, because the fountains below had poured out an ocean, and an ocean takes time to drain. The rain was the start. The deep was the flood.

Picture a house with a burst pipe in the cellar and a storm over the roof at the same time. The storm soaks the roof. The pipe fills the house. If you only mopped the rain, you would still drown. That is the picture this chapter paints: heaven and earth both opened, from the top and from the bottom, and the dry land in the middle had nowhere to hide. No hill was high enough, because the water was not just falling. It was rising.

Who shut the door of the ark?

This is one of the most loved lines in the chapter, and it is only six words in the King James text. Verse 16 ends: "and the LORD shut him in."

Noah did not shut the door. The sons did not shut the door. God shut it. Think about what that means. The door was the one weak place in the boat, the one opening where water could get in. God sealed it Himself. The safety of the ark did not rest on Noah's grip. It rested on God's hand.

There is a second side to it. A shut door also shuts out. Once God shut that door, no one else got in, and the chapter does not record a single knock being answered. The time to come in was while the door stood open, all those years of building and all those seven days of waiting. Mercy had a long open season. Then the season turned. This chapter holds both truths at once, and it does not soften either one: God is patient for a long time, and the door does shut.

Here is your read-through checklist for the chapter, so you can trace the counts yourself with your Bible open:

Checklist:

  • Verse 1: God says "Come," not "Go" — He calls Noah in, to where He is
  • Verses 2–3: clean beasts and birds by sevens, the rest by twos, to keep seed alive
  • Verse 4: seven days to wait, then rain for forty days and forty nights
  • Verses 5–9: Noah does all that God commands, and the animals come two by two
  • Verse 11: the exact day is named — the 600th year, second month, seventeenth day
  • Verse 16: the LORD shut him in — God seals the door, not Noah
  • Verses 19–20: waters rise fifteen cubits over the mountains
  • Verse 24: the waters prevail on the earth 150 days

How deep did the water get?

Verse 19: "And the waters prevailed exceedingly upon the earth; and all the high hills, that were under the whole heaven, were covered." Verse 20 gives the measure: "Fifteen cubits upward did the waters prevail; and the mountains were covered."

Now use the measure we set at the start. A cubit is about a foot and a half. 15 cubits × 1.5 feet = 22.5 feet. The water stood about 22 and a half feet over the tops of the mountains. Why does the depth matter? Think of the ark. In Genesis 6, God set the ark's height at 30 cubits. A boat that size sits deep in the water when it is loaded full of animals and food; a working guess is that near half of it rides under the water line. Half of 30 cubits is 15 cubits. So water 15 cubits over the mountains means the ark, floating with its belly hanging about 15 cubits down, would clear the mountain tops and not tear open on the rocks. The chapter's number is not just big. It is the right size for the boat God designed. The text's own math holds together, measure to measure.

Verse 23 sums up the cost: "And every living substance was destroyed which was upon the face of the ground, both man, and cattle, and the creeping things, and the fowl of the heaven; and they were destroyed from the earth: and Noah only remained alive, and they that were with him in the ark." Only is a heavy word in that line. Only Noah. Only those with him. Everything else that breathed air on dry land was gone. The chapter does not rush past that, and neither should we. Judgment in this book is real, and it is grief, and God Himself grieved first, back in Genesis 6, before He sent it.

What is the bottom line of Genesis 7?

The bottom line is this: God keeps count, God keeps His word, and God keeps His people. He counted the souls, the beasts, the days, and the cubits. He said rain in seven days, and in seven days it rained. He said the ark would save Noah's house, and at the end of the worst chapter in the world so far, Noah's house is alive inside it, with the door shut by God's own hand. The water is still up at the end of the chapter. Genesis 8 will tell how it went down. But Genesis 7 ends with the boat afloat and the promise holding. That is enough to carry into your week: the same God who shuts the door is the God who rides out the storm with the people inside.
