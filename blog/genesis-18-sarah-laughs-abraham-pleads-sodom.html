33 Verses, 3 Visitors, 1 Laugh, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 18 Explained, Sarah Laughs Sodom Plea for You

Abraham sits in the tent door in the heat of the day.

Abraham sits in the tent door in the heat of the day. The heat is thick. The work of the day has stopped. He lifts up his eyes and looks — and there, close by him, three men stand. No sound came first. No call came from the road. They are just there, standing in the bright heat, as if they had been there all along. Abraham is on his feet at once. He runs to meet them and bows himself toward the ground. Just inside the tent door, close behind him, Sarah stands where she can hear. Who are these three men, and why have they come to this tent, of all tents? One of them will speak a word so big that Sarah will laugh at it from behind that door — a laugh she keeps inside herself, sure no one can hear it. What will that laugh mean, and what will it cost her to learn that it was heard?

In one sentence: three men come to Abraham's tent in the heat of the day, one of them promises Sarah a son within the year, Sarah laughs behind the tent door and is heard, and then Abraham stands before the LORD and pleads for Sodom, step by step, from 50 righteous down to 10. An old woman stands just inside a tent door with her hand pressed over her mouth. She is 90 years old. On the other side of the cloth, a guest her husband is feeding under the tree has just said, as if it were a small thing, that she will have a son by this time next year. She laughs — within herself, the book says, the laugh of a woman adding up her own years and her husband's and finding the sum past all bearing. She thinks no one hears a laugh you keep inside. She is wrong. The guest hears it through the tent door, asks her husband why she laughed, and then asks the question this whole chapter turns on: Is any thing too hard for the LORD? This post walks the 33 verses of Genesis 18: the meal under the tree, Sarah's laugh and God's answer to it, the thing God would not hide from Abraham, and the long, bold plea for a city that did not know it was being pled for.

The short answer:

Hold that one sentence in mind as you read: the LORD comes to Abraham's tent with two others, promises Sarah a son at the set time, answers her hidden laugh with a question about His own power, and lets Abraham plead Sodom's case down to 10 righteous. Genesis 18 opens with the LORD appearing to Abraham in the plains of Mamre as he sits in the tent door in the heat of the day. Abraham sees three men standing by him, runs to meet them, bows to the ground, and begs them to stay: water for their feet, rest under the tree, and a morsel of bread. Then he runs — to Sarah for cakes of fine meal, to the herd for a tender calf — and sets butter, milk, and the calf before them, and stands by while they eat. The promise is spoken where Sarah can hear it: at the time appointed, Sarah shall have a son. Sarah, old, with the way of women long ceased for her, laughs within herself. The LORD asks why she laughed, and asks: Is any thing too hard for the LORD? Sarah, afraid, denies it. He answers: Nay, but thou didst laugh. The men then look toward Sodom, and God tells Abraham what He is about to do, for the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great and their sin very grievous. Abraham draws near and pleads: wilt thou destroy the righteous with the wicked? For 50 righteous, God will spare the whole place. Abraham asks again — 45, then 40, then 30, then 20, then 10 — and each time God says He will not destroy it. Then the LORD goes His way, and Abraham returns to his place.

What the video shows: This video reads Genesis 18 in full, in the King James text, one chapter for the day. It opens at Mamre in the heat of the day, with three visitors standing by Abraham's tent and Abraham running to meet them, bowing low, and pressing them to stay for water, rest, and food. It follows the meal as it comes together — Sarah making cakes of fine meal, a tender calf dressed in haste, butter and milk set out under the tree — and then the promise spoken at the tent door: Sarah will have a son within the year. It takes up Sarah's laugh, kept inside herself and still heard, and the LORD's answer: Is any thing too hard for the LORD? Then the tone of the reading turns with the men as they look toward Sodom: the great cry against the city, God's resolve to go down and see, and Abraham standing before the LORD to plead — 50 righteous, 45, 40, 30, 20, and at last 10 — with God agreeing at every step to spare the city for the sake of the few.

Who came to Abraham's tent in the heat of the day?

Verse 1: "And the LORD appeared unto him in the plains of Mamre: and he sat in the tent door in the heat of the day." The plains of Mamre are the same ground where Abram dwelt in Genesis 14, where he built an altar years before. The heat of the day is the dead hour, when work stops and a man sits in the shade of his door. Nothing in the verse shouts. The LORD appeared — and the man of the house is sitting down, resting, in the middle of an ordinary hot day. Heaven's biggest visits in this book rarely come with trumpets. They come at noon, at a spring, by a fire at night, to people sitting in doorways.

Verse 2: "And he lift up his eyes and looked, and, lo, three men stood by him: and when he saw them, he ran to meet them from the tent door, and bowed himself toward the ground." Three men stood by him. Only as the chapter runs on does the page let you see more: one of the three speaks as the LORD Himself, and the other two will walk on to Sodom and be called angels there. For now, hold what Abraham holds: three travelers standing in the heat, and an old man on his feet. He ran — 99 years old, fresh signed with the covenant of last chapter — and he bowed himself toward the ground, the bow of a man who honors the guest before he knows a thing about him.

What did Abraham serve them?

Verse 3 is the ask: "My Lord, if now I have found favour in thy sight, pass not away, I pray thee, from thy servant." Pass not away — do not walk past my door. Verse 4: "Let a little water, I pray you, be fetched, and wash your feet, and rest yourselves under the tree." Verse 5: "And I will fetch a morsel of bread, and comfort ye your hearts; after that ye shall pass on." A morsel is a small bit, a bite. Mark that word, because what comes out of his kitchen is no morsel, and the gap between the word and the meal is the measure of the man's giving: he promises little and serves much.

Verse 6: "And Abraham hastened into the tent unto Sarah, and said, Make ready quickly three measures of fine meal, knead it, and make cakes upon the hearth." Hastened — hurried. Three measures of fine meal is a large batch, worked by Sarah's own hands into cakes. Verse 7: "And Abraham ran unto the herd, and fetcht a calf tender and good, and gave it unto a young man; and he hasted to dress it." Ran, again. A calf, tender and good — the best of the herd, not the one he could spare. Verse 8 sets the table: "And he took butter, and milk, and the calf which he had dressed, and set it before them; and he stood by them under the tree, and they did eat." A feast, built in the heat of the day, at a run, for three men who were walking past. And Abraham does not sit and eat with them. He stood by them, the way a servant stands by a table. The man God called friend serves the meal and stands.

What did the visitor promise Sarah?

Verse 9: "And they said unto him, Where is Sarah thy wife? And he said, Behold, in the tent." They ask for her by name — her new name, Sarah, given by God in the talk of last chapter, a name no stranger on the road should know. Verse 10: "And he said, I will certainly return unto thee according to the time of life; and, lo, Sarah thy wife shall have a son. And Sarah heard it in the tent door, which was behind him." One of the three now speaks in the first person of the LORD: I will certainly return. The time of life means about this time next year, the set time God named in Genesis 17. And Sarah heard it — placed by the book on purpose in the tent door, behind him, close enough to hear every word. This time the promise is spoken at her own door, in her own hearing, with her own name in it.

Verse 11 lays the facts out flat: "Now Abraham and Sarah were old and well stricken in age; and it ceased to be with Sarah after the manner of women." Well stricken in age means far gone in years. And the way of women — the course by which a woman can bear — had ceased for Sarah. The book says the plain thing plainly: by every law of the body, this son cannot come. It said the same of Abraham in the last chapter: a child to a man of 100. Both halves of the impossibility are on the page before the promise is tested, so no reader can soften the promise into a likely thing. It is not likely. That is the point of it.

Why did Sarah laugh, and what did God say?

Verse 12: "Therefore Sarah laughed within herself, saying, After I am waxed old shall I have pleasure, my lord being old also?" Within herself. This laugh is not spoken; it rises up inside her before she can stop it. Waxed old means grown old, worn with years. She does the math the way Abraham did the math in the last chapter: the ages, the years, the closed door of her own body. Her laugh is his laugh's twin. He laughed on his face before God and was not rebuked. She laughs inside herself behind a tent door — and is answered. The answer is not a scolding. It is a question, and it is aimed at the math, not at the woman.

Verse 13: "And the LORD said unto Abraham, Wherefore did Sarah laugh, saying, Shall I of a surety bear a child, which am old?" Wherefore means why. God names the hidden thing out loud: the laugh she kept inside, and the words she said inside, which no man heard. She is answered as if her inside words had been spoken at the table like a guest's fair question. Verse 14 is the center of the chapter: "Is any thing too hard for the LORD? At the time appointed I will return unto thee, according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son." It is a question with one answer, and the answer is no. Not the age of the man. Not the closed womb of the woman. Not 24 years of waiting. Nothing named in this tale is too hard for the LORD, and the proof will not be argued — it will be born. At the time appointed. The laugh changes nothing about the date.

Verse 15: "Then Sarah denied, saying, I laughed not; for she was afraid. And he said, Nay; but thou didst laugh." She was afraid. A hidden thing of hers has been spoken at the table by a guest who knew her new name, and fear does what fear does: it denies. And the answer is firm and short and not cruel: Nay; but thou didst laugh. God will not let the denial stand, and He will not crush her for it either. She did laugh — and she shall have a son. Both sentences are true, and the second one is bigger. The boy will carry the laugh in his name for ever, Isaac, he laughs — her laugh, and his father's, kept by God as a name and not as a charge.

Here is your read-through checklist for the visit and the laugh, so you can trace it with your Bible open:

Checklist:

  • Verse 2: three men stood by him — Abraham ran to meet them and bowed to the ground
  • Verses 4–5: water for their feet, rest under the tree, a morsel of bread promised
  • Verses 6–8: three measures of fine meal, a calf tender and good, butter and milk — and Abraham stood by them as they ate
  • Verse 10: I will certainly return according to the time of life, and Sarah shall have a son
  • Verse 12: Sarah laughed within herself — after I am waxed old shall I have pleasure?
  • Verse 14: Is any thing too hard for the LORD? At the time appointed, Sarah shall have a son
  • Verse 15: I laughed not — Nay; but thou didst laugh
  • Verses 23–32: Abraham pleads for Sodom — 50, 45, 40, 30, 20, 10

What would God not hide from Abraham?

Verse 16 turns the chapter on its hinge: "And the men rose up from thence, and looked toward Sodom: and Abraham went with them to bring them on the way." The meal is over. The men rise and look toward Sodom — a look with weight in it. Abraham walks with them a stretch, as a good host does. And on that walk, verse 17 opens a door into God's own counsel: "And the LORD said, Shall I hide from Abraham that thing which I do, seeing that Abraham shall surely become a great and mighty nation, and all the nations of the earth shall be blessed in him?" The man through whom all nations will be blessed is not to be kept in the dark about judgment falling on a nation. Verse 19 gives God's ground for trusting him with it: "For I know him, that he will command his children and his household after him, and they shall keep the way of the LORD, to do justice and judgment." I know him. The way of the LORD is named: justice and judgment, doing right and judging right.

Verse 20: "And the LORD said, Because the cry of Sodom and Gomorrah is great, and because their sin is very grievous." The cry is what rises from people being crushed — the sound a city's wrongs make when they reach heaven. There are victims, and their cry has come up. Verse 21: "I will go down now, and see whether they have done altogether according to the cry of it, which is come unto me; and if not, I will know." The Judge of all the earth does not strike on rumor. He goes down. He sees for Himself. The two travelers walking on to Sodom are that going down — the seeing, done in person, before any judgment falls. Abraham is not pleading with a God who has already swung the sword. He is pleading with a God who has stopped on the road to tell His friend what He is doing.

How did Abraham plead for Sodom?

Verse 22: "And the men turned their faces from thence, and went toward Sodom: but Abraham stood yet before the LORD." The two go on. Abraham stands. Verse 23: "And Abraham drew near, and said, Wilt thou also destroy the righteous with the wicked?" Drew near — he steps closer to God to make this plea, not back. His question is the question of the whole scene: will the Judge sweep away good and bad in one stroke? Righteous means those in the right, the ones who do right. Abraham's plea is not for Sodom's sin to be excused. It is for the righteous inside Sodom not to be treated as the wicked. He has kin in that city — Lot dwells there — but Abraham never names Lot, not once in six pleadings. He pleads for the righteous, whoever they are. The plea is bigger than family. It is a plea about what kind of Judge God is.

Verse 24 sets the first number: "Peradventure there be fifty righteous within the city: wilt thou also destroy and not spare the place for the fifty righteous that are therein?" Peradventure means perhaps, if it may be. Fifty. Verse 25 is the boldest line a man speaks to God in this whole book so far, and it is bold because it argues from God's own nature: "That be far from thee to do after this manner, to slay the righteous with the wicked: and that the righteous should be as the wicked, that be far from thee: Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" Abraham does not beg God to be other than He is. He begs God to be exactly who He is. That is the ground the whole plea stands on, and it is bedrock.

Verse 26: "And the LORD said, If I find in Sodom fifty righteous within the city, then I will spare all the place for their sakes." Spare all the place. Not pull the 50 out and burn the rest — spare the whole city, wicked and all, for the sake of the 50 righteous in it. The few would carry the many. The righteous would be a shield over the very neighbors who mock them. God says yes to that at the first asking, in full. And then Abraham, dust and ashes, asks again.

Why did Abraham keep asking, down to 10?

Verse 27: "And Abraham answered and said, Behold now, I have taken upon me to speak unto the Lord, which am but dust and ashes." Dust and ashes — Abraham's own name for himself in this talk. He knows the size of what he is doing, and still he speaks. Verse 28: 45 — "If I find there forty and five, I will not destroy it." Verse 29: 40 — "I will not do it for forty's sake." Verse 30: "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak: Peradventure there shall thirty be found there. And he said, I will not do it, if I find thirty there." Verse 31: 20 — "I will not destroy it for twenty's sake." Verse 32: "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak yet but this once: Peradventure ten shall be found there. And he said, I will not destroy it for ten's sake."

Count the steps: 50, 45, 40, 30, 20, 10. Six times Abraham asks. Six times God says yes, in full, at once. Think of it like a man at a market stall, haggling the price down coin by coin — only here the goods are a city's life, the seller is God, and the buyer pays nothing but boldness and dust-and-ashes humility, held in one hand each. The book lets you watch a man learn, in real time, that God's mercy is wider than his asking. He stops at 10. The text does not say God stopped him. Abraham stops — perhaps because 10 is the size of a household, the smallest count of a righteous remnant he can picture, perhaps because the lesson is learned: the Judge he feared might sweep too wide is in fact sparing wider than the plea.

Verse 33: "And the LORD went his way, as soon as he had left communing with Abraham: and Abraham returned unto his place." Communing means talking together. The talk ends the way a talk between friends ends: each goes his way. Abraham returns to Mamre, to Sarah, to wait for the set time. He has done what a man can do for Sodom: stood in the road and pled the Judge's own nature back to Him, six times, down to 10. What he cannot know, walking home, is how few righteous Sodom holds. The reader who goes on to Genesis 19 will find out — and will find Abraham's plea at work there in a form no one foresaw: not a city spared, but a man pulled out by the hand, because God remembered Abraham.

Here is the cost beat of this chapter, and it is not Abraham's cost first — it is the cost hidden inside the mercy. If 10 righteous would have spared the whole city, then the many would have lived under a covering they did not earn and would never know about. Sodom is about to show the world what it holds instead. And for Abraham, the cost is the walk home with no answer about Lot. He pled to 10 and stopped, and the book does not tell us he slept easy. He has a son coming by promise and a nephew living in a city under a cry, and both facts ride home with him in the heat. Faith in this chapter is not a warm feeling. It is a feast served at a run, a laugh answered with a promise, and a plea made for people who would have laughed at the man making it.

What is Genesis 18's bottom line?

Come back to the old woman behind the tent door, hand over her mouth. Her laugh was heard, answered, and turned into a name the child will carry. And come back to her husband on the road, dust and ashes, haggling heaven down to 10 for a city that was not his. So the whole tale still fits in that first one sentence: the LORD eats at Abraham's table, promises Sarah a son, and lets Abraham plead for Sodom down to 10 righteous. The bottom line is this: nothing is too hard for the LORD — not a son for a woman of 90, and not mercy for a city with 10 righteous in it. The same God who hears a laugh through a tent door hears a plea on a road, and answers both out of who He is. The next chapter walks into Sodom to see what the cry has made of it, and to find out whether 10 righteous can be found.
