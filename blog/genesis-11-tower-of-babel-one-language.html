9 Verses, 1 Tongue, 1 Tower, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 11 Explained, the Tower of Babel for You

In one sentence: the builders of Babel spoke one tongue and raised one tower to make a name for themselves and stay together in one place, but God mixed their words and scattered them over the whole earth.

In one sentence: the builders of Babel spoke one tongue and raised one tower to make a name for themselves and stay together in one place, but God mixed their words and scattered them over the whole earth. A man stands on the flat plain with a hot brick in his hands, and he cannot make the man next to him hear one plain word. An hour ago they spoke the same tongue. They had the same words for brick, for lift, for more. Now his work mate opens his mouth and out comes a sound this man has never heard in his life. He tries again, slow. He points at the brick. He points up at the tower, half built, its top lost in the heat haze. Nothing works. The two men look at each other, and then they look at the crowd, and the whole plain is full of men doing the same thing: talking, pointing, shaking their heads. No one has hit them. No wall has fallen. The work has just stopped, all at once, in the middle of a hot day. What stopped it was not a storm or a king. It was a word they could no longer share. This post walks the nine verses that tell how men got to that plain, what they meant to build there, and why God Himself came down to see it.

The short answer:

Hold that one sentence in mind as you read: one people, one tongue, one tower built for a name and for staying put, and one God who breaks the plan by mixing the words that held it up. Genesis 11 opens with the whole earth speaking one language. The people move east, find a plain in the land of Shinar, and settle there. They learn to bake bricks hard and to join them with slime, and they set out to build a city and a tower whose top may reach to heaven. Their own words give their aim: to make a name for themselves, and to keep from being scattered over the earth. God comes down to see the city and the tower. He confounds their language, so they cannot understand each other, and He scatters them over the face of all the earth. They leave off building the city, and its name is called Babel. The rest of the chapter slows down to one line of names, from Shem to Abram, and ends with a family on the road from Ur, stopped at Haran.

It ends with the two roads the book now puts in front of you: the name men tried to make, and the name God is about to give.

What did the whole earth sound like at the start?

Verse 1: "And the whole earth was of one language, and of one speech." One language means one tongue for all men. One speech means the same set of words. There was no need for anyone to translate, and no man alive could hide his plans in words his neighbor could not follow. Keep that fact in your hand, because the whole chapter turns on it. The gift that lets men work as one body is the very gift God will touch at the end.

Verse 2: "And it came to pass, as they journeyed from the east, that they found a plain in the land of Shinar; and they dwelt there." They were on the move, as God had told Noah's sons to be. Back in Genesis 9, God blessed Noah and his sons and said, "Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth." Replenish here means fill it up again. The plan was spread: families moving out, filling the earth. But at Shinar the move stops. They found a plain, a wide flat place with room for all, and they dwelt there. Dwelt is a settle word. The first quiet turn of the chapter is not the tower. It is the staying.

Think of it like a troop of ants at a crack in a path. The line is meant to keep moving, each ant with its load, out to the whole yard. Then the line finds a warm flat stone, and the ants pile up on the stone and stop. Nothing has gone wrong yet. The stone is fine. But the job was the yard, not the stone. Shinar is the warm flat stone. The people were made to fill the earth, and they chose one plain and stayed.

How did they build without stone?

Verse 3: "And they said one to another, Go to, let us make brick, and burn them throughly." Go to is an old way of saying come on, let us start. The plain of Shinar had no good stone to cut, so they made their own: mud shaped into bricks and burned hard in fire, throughly, all the way through. This is real craft. A sun-dried brick crumbles. A burned brick lasts. These men were not fools. They were skilled, and their skill is part of the tale.

The verse goes on: "And they had brick for stone, and slime had they for morter." Morter, spelled mortar in newer print, is the soft mix that holds bricks or stones in a wall. They had no lime mix, so they used slime, a sticky tar-like stuff from the ground, the same kind of stuff Noah used to seal the ark. Brick for stone, slime for mortar: a full swap of what the land gave for what their hands could make. On a flat plain with no rock, they had found a way to build as high as they liked.

Here is the same point in a kitchen picture. A cook with no flour in the house can still make a flat cake from meal and water if she knows her craft. The lack of one thing does not stop a skilled hand; it just sends the hand to a swap. The men of Shinar made their swap well. No verse in this chapter blames their bricks. The bricks were good bricks. What God answers is not the craft. It is the aim the craft was bent to serve, and the builders state that aim out loud in the next verse, in their own words.

What did the builders say they wanted?

Verse 4: "And they said, Go to, let us build us a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven; and let us make us a name, lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth." Read their plan in three parts, because they give it in three parts.

First, a city and a tower, whose top may reach unto heaven. The tower is a stair work of baked brick, meant to rise as high as men could pile it. The text does not say they meant to climb up and fight God, and we should not add that to the page. Later readers have alleged that the builders meant to storm heaven itself, but the verse itself gives a plainer aim, and we stay with what the verse says. What the verse says is height, a city, and then the two reasons.

Second, let us make us a name. A name here means fame: a name men speak of, a mark that lasts. In Genesis 10 you just read a list of names God kept in His book. Here men want to make their own name, by their own work, for their own praise. Hold that line up against the very next chapter, where God says to Abram, "I will make thy name great." Men at Babel grab for a name. Abram is given one. The book sets the two side by side on purpose, one chapter apart.

Third, lest we be scattered abroad. Lest means so that it will not happen. They knew the word of God to fill the earth, and they built a wall against it. The city was a knot to hold them in one place. The tower was a mark they could see from far off, so no one would stray. Their fear was the very thing God had blessed: men spread over the whole earth. So the plan, in plain words, was this: stay, rise, and be famed, all in one place, against the word that sent them out.

Why did God come down to see the tower?

Verse 5: "And the LORD came down to see the city and the tower, which the children of men builded." Came down is the book's own way of speaking, and it carries a dry smile. Men built up to reach heaven, and the text says God came down to look at their high work. The tower that felt so tall from the plain was a thing God stooped to view. You do not need to add a word to feel the scale of that. The tallest work of all men, taken in one look, from above.

There is a court sense in it too. In this book, God comes down to see before He acts, the way a just judge views the thing with his own eyes before he gives a ruling. He did the same at Sodom, later in this same book, when He said He would go down and see whether the cry of the city was true. God is never in a rush, and He never rules on hearsay. The coming down says the act that follows is fair, weighed, and seen.

Verse 6: "And the LORD said, Behold, the people is one, and they have all one language; and this they begin to do: and now nothing will be restrained from them, which they have imagined to do." Restrained means held back. God names the true strength of the plain: not the bricks, but the oneness. One people, one tongue, one plan. United men can do near all they set their minds to, and God says so plainly. The risk was not this one tower. The risk was a whole race locked as one, bent on a name of its own, with no bar left on what it might next imagine. The tower was only what they "begin to do."

What did God do, and why that and not more?

Verse 7: "Go to, let us go down, and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech." Hear the echo. The builders said, "Go to, let us build." God answers in their own form: "Go to, let us go down." Confound means mix up so it cannot be followed. God does not knock the tower down. He does not strike the builders. He touches the one gift their oneness stood on: their shared speech. Take away the shared words, and the shared work stops on its own. No man can lay a course of bricks when he cannot ask for a brick.

Think of a work crew on a roof when the radio dies and each man is on a line no one else can hear. The tools are still good. The wood is still cut. But the calls for lift, hold, and pass no longer land, and wise men climb down before someone gets hurt. That is Babel in one picture. The stop was not a crash. It was a silence where the orders used to be. And note the mercy in the shape of it: not one life is lost in this chapter. The flood judged by water. Babel is judged by words alone, and every man walks away alive, just unable to stay.

Here is your read-through checklist for the tower part of the chapter, so you can trace it with your Bible open:

Checklist:

  • Verse 1: the whole earth is of one language and one speech
  • Verse 2: the people journey, find a plain in the land of Shinar, and dwell there
  • Verse 3: brick for stone and slime for mortar — burned bricks, made by craft
  • Verse 4: a city and a tower, a name for themselves, lest they be scattered
  • Verses 5–6: the LORD comes down to see, and names their oneness as their strength
  • Verse 7: let us go down and confound their language
  • Verses 8–9: they are scattered, they leave off building, and the place is called Babel

Verse 8: "So the LORD scattered them abroad from thence upon the face of all the earth: and they left off to build the city." The thing they feared most is the thing that came, and it came as the cure, not just the cost. Scattered is the very word they built against. Yet the scattering is God's first plan coming back on track: men spread over the earth, in families and tongues, as the lists in Genesis 10 already showed. Verse 9: "Therefore is the name of it called Babel; because the LORD did there confound the language of all the earth." Babel sounds like the Hebrew word for confound, balal, to mix. They wanted a name. They got one. The name they got was Confusion.

Why does the chapter end with a list of names?

From verse 10 the pace changes. "These are the generations of Shem." Generations here means the line of a man's seed, name by name. Shem was a hundred years old, and begat Arphaxad two years after the flood. Then the names march: Arphaxad, Salah, Eber, Peleg, Reu, Serug, Nahor, Terah. Ten names from Shem to Abram, the same count as the ten names from Adam to Noah in Genesis 5. The book is built with care. Two lines of ten frame the flood and Babel, and both lines end with a man God will speak to.

Why spend half a chapter on names and ages after the tower? Because God is showing you where He works next. The crowd at Babel tried to make one big name and lost it. God keeps a small line of names, one son at a time, in quiet years no crowd ever saw. The ages shrink as the line runs: Shem lives 600 years, and by Terah's time the count is 205. The long lives of the first chapters are drawing down toward the spans we know. The world is settling into its new shape, and the camera of the book narrows from the whole earth, to one line, to one house.

Verse 29 names Abram's wife: Sarai. Verse 30 says the hard thing plain: "But Sarai was barren; she had no child." Barren means she could not bear a child. The line God has just spent nine verses building now rests on a house with no child in it. That is not a flaw in the tale. It is the hook the whole rest of the book hangs on. God does not pick the house that has all it needs. He picks the house that lacks the one thing the promise will need most.

Where does the chapter leave Terah's house?

Verse 31: "And Terah took Abram his son, and Lot the son of Haran his son's son, and Sarai his daughter in law, his son Abram's wife; and they went forth with them from Ur of the Chaldees, to go into the land of Canaan; and they came unto Haran, and dwelt there." Ur of the Chaldees was a rich city far to the east. Terah set out for Canaan, the land God will soon name to Abram. But the house stopped at Haran, a town on the way, and dwelt there. The same settle word as Shinar. They meant to go. They stopped halfway, and they stayed.

Verse 32: "And the days of Terah were two hundred and five years: and Terah died in Haran." The chapter ends with a death in the halfway town. The road to Canaan stands open and untaken. The line of names has reached Abram. His wife has no child. His father is dead in a place that was meant to be a stop, not a home. If you stopped reading here, you would say the tale has run into a wall. That wall is exactly where the next chapter starts, with God speaking into it: get thee out. Babel ends with men scattered by force. Genesis 12 will show one man sent out by a promise, and glad to go.

Here is the cost beat of this chapter, and it cuts two ways. The men of Shinar lost their city, but they kept their lives, their families, and their craft, and their seed fills the lists of nations. What they truly lost was the one thing they most wanted to keep: the single name and the single place. A careless read can lose the same shape in small. If you read Babel as a tale about a tall tower, you miss that the tower is never even said to fall. The loss was speech, place, and name, and the gain, though they could not see it, was the earth filled at last, as God had said from the start.

What is Genesis 11's bottom line?

Come back to the man on the plain with the hot brick in his hands. He did nothing cruel that day. He baked good bricks and laid them true. What he could not do was make his own name stand and keep his world in one place, because God had a wider plan than one plain. So the whole tale still fits in that first one sentence: builders raised a tower to make a name and stay together, and God scattered them by mixing the tongue that made them one. The bottom line is this: men grab for a name, and lose it; God gives a name, and it lasts. The chapter that starts with the whole earth speaking as one ends with one small house, stopped on a road, with no child and no home of its own. That house is the one God chooses. The tower stands half built to this day in the tale, a mark of what men begin. The line of names is still being read, a mark of what God carries through.
