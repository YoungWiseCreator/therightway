10 Names, 930 Years, 1 KJV Text: Genesis 5 Explained, the Genealogy and Enoch Your Family Reads Slow

Most readers skip Genesis 5. It looks like a list.

Most readers skip Genesis 5. It looks like a list. Names, years, more names, more years. But read it slow and the list starts to sound like a bell. A man lives. A son is born. The man dies. Then it rings again. And one time, in the middle, the bell does not ring. This post walks the whole list with you, name by name, so you can hear what the chapter is doing.

The short answer:

Genesis 5 is the book of the generations of Adam. It runs the line from Adam through Seth down to Noah, 10 names in all. Each man lives a very long life, has a son, and dies. The words and he died come again and again, 8 times. One man breaks the pattern: Enoch walked with God, and he was not, for God took him. The chapter ends with Noah, whose name speaks of comfort, born to Lamech in a world still under the curse on the ground.

What the video shows: The video reads Genesis 5 in full, the whole list of names from Adam to Noah, and it slows down on the parts most readers rush past. It shows the same line at the end of almost every life, and he died, rung like a bell over Adam, Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalaleel, and Jared. It lays the long lives side by side, from Adam at 930 years to Methuselah at 969 years, the oldest man in the Bible. Then it stops at Enoch, the one man in the list who did not die, who walked with God for 300 years and then was no more, because God took him. It ends with Lamech's words over his baby son Noah, and with the comfort his name holds.

What is a genealogy, and why does the book keep one?

A genealogy is a list of a family line. It names a father, then his son, then that son's son, down through the years. It is how a people keeps track of who they come from. Think of it as a rope with knots. Each knot is a man. The rope runs from Adam all the way to Noah, and past Noah it will run on through the rest of the book.

Genesis 5 opens with its own title: This is the book of the generations of Adam. Generations means the line of sons and the story of what came from a man. The book of Genesis uses this kind of title more than once. Each time it starts, a new stretch of the story starts. Here, the stretch is the line of Seth.

That word Seth matters. Genesis 4 ended with two lines in view. Cain's line built a city and sang a song of blood. Seth's line began to call on the name of the LORD. Genesis 5 follows Seth's line. It does not run Cain's line again. The book has picked the line it will carry, the line that calls on God, and it walks that line name by name for 10 names, till it lands on Noah.

The chapter also reaches back to the start. Verse 1 and 2 say God created man in His own likeness, male and female, and blessed them, and called their name Adam. Likeness means a true copy in form and nature, the way a son can have his father's face. Genesis 1 said man was made in the image of God. Genesis 5 says it again, right at the top of the list, so you do not forget whose face this family bears, even after the fall, even under the curse, even as each man in the list dies.

Then verse 3 adds the sad twin of that fact. Adam has a son in his own likeness, after his image, and calls him Seth. Adam passes his own face down. But Adam is now a man under death. So the likeness of God and the mark of the fall run down the same rope, knot by knot. That is the whole chapter in one thought: God's image, carried by men who die.

How long did these men live, and what is the pattern of each life?

The lives in Genesis 5 are long. Very long. Adam lives 930 years. Seth lives 912. Jared lives 962. Methuselah lives 969, the longest life in the whole Bible. A year is one full trip of the seasons, spring to spring. These men live through more than 9 sets of 100 years each. Adam lives long enough that, by the count of years in this list, he is still alive when Lamech, Noah's father, is born. The first man could have told the father of Noah about the garden with his own mouth. The list is not just long. It links the start of the world to the days of the flood in a few lives that touch each other.

Each life is told in the same three beats. Beat one: a man lives a set count of years and has a named son. Beat two: he lives more years after that son, and has other sons and daughters. Beat three: all his days are summed, and he died.

Run the math on Adam and you see the shape. Adam lives 130 years, then Seth is born. 130 plus 800 more years is 930. All the days of Adam were 930 years, and he died. The same shape holds for Seth: 105 plus 807 is 912. For Enosh: 90 plus 815 is 905. The counts are plain, and the text gives you both parts and the sum, so you can check each one with your own hand. Do that with your kids. Add the two parts. See if the sum is right. It is a good way to read a list slow: with a pencil.

But do not let the long counts hide the last two words of each life. And he died. God told Adam in Genesis 2 that in the day he ate from the one tree, he would surely die. Adam did not drop dead that same day. He lived 930 years. But the word held. Death came into the world and it does not skip a name in this list. King or shepherd, 930 years or 777, the bell rings the same. The long lives show God's patience. The same end on each life shows God's word is true.

One more plain note. The list names one son per man, the son who carries the line. It also says each man had other sons and daughters. So the world fills up fast in these years, with many families the list does not name. The list is a rope, not a census. A census is a count of every person. This list does not count every person. It traces one line on purpose.

Some of the names carry sense you can hear. Enosh is a word for man, man in his weak and mortal side. Mortal means sure to die. Mahalaleel holds the name of God in it and means praise of God. Enoch means dedicated, set apart for a task. Noah's name sounds like the word for comfort and rest, and his father's words in verse 29 say just that.

Do not rush past the sameness of the list. The sameness is the point. Son after son, the line holds. No king in it. No war in it. Just births, long years, and deaths, one after the next, while the world grows more wild around them, as Genesis 6 will show. God keeps the line alive through plain years and plain names. Most of the men in this list do nothing the book stops to praise. They live, they raise sons, they die in faith that the promise of Genesis 3, the seed of the woman, will still come down this rope. And it does. The rope holds.

Who is Enoch, and why does his part sound different?

Enoch is the seventh name in the line, the seventh from Adam. Seventh means number 7 in the count. His part of the list starts the same way. Enoch lived 65 years and had a son, Methuselah. Then the text changes key.

Verse 22: Enoch walked with God after he had Methuselah 300 years, and had sons and daughters. Walked with God means he lived his days close to God, in step with Him, the way two friends walk a road side by side and talk as they go. It does not mean a walk on one day. It means 300 years of days, at home, at work, with his kids, in step with God.

Verse 23 sums his life: all the days of Enoch were 365 years. That is the shortest life in the list. 365 is also the count of days in a full year of the sun. His whole life is one round year next to the 900-year lives around him.

Then verse 24, and the bell does not ring. Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him. He was not. He was not there anymore. No and he died. In a list where death ends every part, the lack of those words shouts. God took him. Enoch's life on earth ends not with a grave the text names, but with God taking him to Himself.

Here is the hardest idea in the chapter, so hear it twice.

First, think of the bell at a church or a school. It rings at the end of each class, all day, the same sound. Ring, ring, ring. Then one hour, the bell does not ring, and the whole room looks up. The quiet is the loudest part of the day. Genesis 5 is that room. And he died, and he died, and he died — then quiet, at Enoch. The quiet tells you death is not the last word God has.

Second, think of a man walking with his father on a long road home. They walk all day, side by side, past fields and towns. At dusk, they are so close to home that the son does not turn off at a side path. He just walks in the door with his father. There is no sad parting on the road, because the walk itself ends at home. Enoch's walk with God is like that. He walked with God so long and so close that the walk just went home. No grave is named, because the road ended at God's door.

Both pictures hold the same truth the text states in six plain words: God took him. In the middle of the death list, God shows that He can take a man past death. Hope is planted in the list itself, not just at its end.

How does the chapter end, and what should you watch for next?

After Enoch, the list goes on. Methuselah, his son, lives 969 years, longer than any man in the book, and still his part ends: and he died. Even the longest life ends. Then Lamech, 777 years, and he died. The bell rings to the end of the list. But Lamech gives the chapter its only speech, and it is a speech over a baby.

Verse 29: Lamech names his son Noah, and says, This same shall comfort us concerning our work and toil of our hands, because of the ground which the LORD hath cursed. Toil is hard, sore work. Lamech works the same cursed ground Adam worked. His back aches. His hands hurt. And he looks at his baby son and hopes out loud: this one will bring us comfort in the hard work, under the curse.

Comfort here does not mean the curse is lifted. Lamech does not say that. He hopes for rest and relief inside the hard days, a man through whom God will give the world a new start. He is more right than he knows. Noah will build the ark, and through Noah the human race will pass through the flood and start again on washed ground. The hope named at a birth in Genesis 5 is the story Genesis 6 and the chapters after it will tell.

Verse 32 ends the chapter: Noah was 500 years old, and Noah begat Shem, Ham, and Japheth. Begat means he became the father of. Three sons. The rope of the list now has three strands, and the next chapter opens with the world grown great in number and great in sin. The line is in place. The man is in place. The comfort Lamech hoped for will be tested by the worst days the world has seen.

Read the whole chapter one more time, all 32 verses, out loud if you can. Count the times you hear and he died. You should count 8. Count the names. You should count 10. Then stop at verse 24 and read it one more time by itself. In a chapter built to show that God's word about death is true, one man walks off the page with God. Both facts stand. That is why this list is worth a slow read.

The bottom line

Genesis 5 is a list with a bell in it. Ten names run from Adam to Noah. Eight times the text says and he died, and God's word about death proves true on every name. But Enoch walked with God, and God took him, and the bell went quiet once — a sign that death is not God's last word. The chapter ends with a baby named for comfort. Read all 32 verses slow, in order, and let the KJV text say what it says, no more and no less.
